Climate Change: Our Planet's Biggest Challenge

Read the passage carefully, then answer the questions below. Your progress bar fills in as you answer.

If you ask a scientist to name the biggest challenge facing our planet today, most of them will give the same answer: climate change. You have probably heard this term many times, in the news, at school, or on social media, but what does it actually mean? In simple words, climate change refers to long-term changes in the Earth's temperature and weather patterns. While the climate has always changed slowly over thousands of years, scientists agree that the planet is now warming much faster than at any point in recorded history, and human activity is the main reason why.

To understand climate change, it helps to understand something called the "greenhouse effect." Earth's atmosphere contains certain gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, which trap heat from the sun, similar to how the glass walls of a greenhouse keep plants warm inside. This natural process is actually necessary for life on Earth; without it, our planet would be far too cold for humans, animals, and plants to survive. The problem is that human activities, especially burning fossil fuels like coal, oil, and gas for energy, have released enormous amounts of extra greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. This has made the natural greenhouse effect much stronger than it should be, causing the planet's average temperature to rise.

The effects of this warming can already be seen around the world. Glaciers and ice sheets in places like Antarctica and Greenland are melting at a faster rate than ever recorded, which causes sea levels to rise and threatens coastal cities and small island nations. Extreme weather events, such as powerful hurricanes, long droughts, and unusually intense heatwaves, are becoming more frequent and more severe in many regions. Wildfires, which naturally occur in some ecosystems, are now burning larger areas for longer periods of time in places like California, Australia, and the Mediterranean. Scientists warn that if global temperatures continue to rise at the current rate, these problems will only become more serious in the coming decades.

Climate change does not only affect the environment; it also affects people's everyday lives, especially in vulnerable communities. Farmers in some regions are struggling because unpredictable weather makes it harder to grow crops successfully, which can lead to food shortages and rising prices. In coastal areas, rising sea levels threaten homes, schools, and entire towns, forcing some families to relocate to safer areas. This growing group of people, sometimes called "climate refugees," is expected to increase significantly in the coming years if serious action is not taken. Importantly, research shows that countries and communities that have contributed the least to climate change often suffer its worst effects, which raises serious questions about fairness and responsibility.

Despite these worrying facts, there is also reason for hope. Around the world, countries, cities, and companies are taking action to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Renewable energy sources, such as solar panels and wind turbines, are becoming cheaper and more efficient every year, allowing more countries to reduce their dependence on fossil fuels. Electric cars, which do not burn gasoline, are becoming more common on roads in many countries, including Turkey. Governments are also creating new laws and international agreements, such as the Paris Agreement, in which many countries promised to work together to limit global warming and reduce their carbon emissions.

Individual actions matter too, even though they might seem small compared to global problems. Simple choices, such as using public transportation instead of driving alone, reducing food waste, recycling, or using less electricity, can add up to a meaningful difference when millions of people make similar choices. Schools and universities are also playing an important role by teaching students about sustainability and encouraging them to think critically about their own habits and consumption. Many young people around the world have become active voices in the climate movement, organizing protests, starting environmental clubs, and pushing political leaders to take stronger action.

Climate change can feel like an overwhelming problem, especially for young people who did not create it but will live with its consequences for most of their lives. However, experts emphasize that the situation is not hopeless. With continued scientific innovation, stronger international cooperation, and the involvement of younger generations who care deeply about the future of the planet, meaningful progress is still possible. Understanding climate change is the first step; the next step, many believe, belongs to the generation growing up today — the generation that will decide what kind of planet comes next.
