Space Exploration: Humanity's Next Giant Leap

Read the passage carefully, then answer the questions below. Your progress bar fills in as you answer.

On July 20, 1969, millions of people around the world sat in front of their television screens and watched something that had never happened before: a human being walked on the Moon. Astronaut Neil Armstrong stepped onto the grey, dusty surface and said words that are still famous today: "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." For a moment, the entire planet felt connected by one incredible achievement. More than fifty years later, humanity is preparing for an even bigger adventure — sending astronauts back to the Moon, and eventually, to Mars.

Space exploration did not begin with the Moon landing. It started years earlier, during a period of history called the "Space Race," when two powerful countries, the United States and the Soviet Union, competed to see who could achieve more in space. In 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, shocking the world and starting a new era of technology. Four years later, in 1961, a Soviet astronaut named Yuri Gagarin became the first human to travel into space, orbiting the Earth in just 108 minutes. These early missions were dangerous and full of unknown risks, but they proved that space travel was possible.

Today, space exploration is no longer only about competition between two countries; it has become a global effort involving many nations and even private companies. The International Space Station, often called the ISS, is a huge laboratory that orbits Earth at a speed of about 28,000 kilometers per hour. Astronauts from different countries live and work together on the ISS, conducting scientific experiments that cannot be done on Earth, such as studying how the human body changes without gravity. This research helps scientists prepare for longer space journeys in the future, including a possible trip to Mars, which could take astronauts more than six months just to arrive.

One of the biggest changes in modern space exploration is the involvement of private companies. In the past, only governments had the money and technology to send rockets into space. Now, private companies build and launch their own rockets, some of which can be used again instead of being thrown away after one flight. This idea, called "reusable rockets," has made space travel much cheaper than before. Some companies are even planning to send ordinary people, not just professional astronauts, on short trips to space as tourists — something that seemed impossible only twenty years ago.

But why do humans spend so much time, money, and energy exploring space? One important reason is scientific discovery. By studying other planets, moons, and stars, scientists can learn more about how our own planet was formed and how it might change in the future. Space telescopes, like powerful cameras that orbit far above Earth's atmosphere, have taken incredible photographs of galaxies that are billions of light-years away, helping us understand the size and age of the universe. Some missions search for signs of water or even life on other planets, such as Mars, which scientists believe may have had liquid water millions of years ago.

Another reason is survival. Some scientists argue that exploring other planets is important for the long-term future of humanity. Earth faces many challenges, including climate change, natural disasters, and the possibility of an asteroid impact. Although no one expects humans to leave Earth completely anytime soon, having the technology and knowledge to live on another planet, such as Mars, could one day protect our species from complete disaster. Building a small human settlement on Mars is no longer just an idea from science fiction; space agencies and private companies are actively researching how this could become reality within the next few decades.

Space exploration also inspires people in a way that few other fields can. It pushes engineers, scientists, and even artists to imagine what is possible beyond the limits of our own planet. Many of today's most important technologies, including satellite communication, GPS navigation, and even some medical equipment, were first developed because of research related to space missions. For students today, space exploration represents one of the biggest and most exciting frontiers of human knowledge, a field where mathematics, physics, biology, and engineering all come together for one enormous shared goal.

Humanity's journey into space is far from finished. In the coming years, new missions will attempt to land astronauts on the Moon again, build long-term Moon bases, and eventually send the first humans to walk on Mars. Whether or not this generation of students becomes astronauts themselves, they will certainly live through some of the most important moments in the history of space exploration — a story that began with one small step, and is still being written today.
